Strategic assessmentPrepared for the Chairman, Tata Sons26 August 2026

The TCS question

Eighty-seven per cent of Tata Sons’ dividend income comes from one company, and that company has just reported the first revenue decline of its listed life. This is an assessment of how much of that is the cycle, how much is structural, and what it means for the group’s ability to fund everything else.

01 · The judgement

Not a trough. A repricing.

The honest reading is that TCS is facing three separate problems at once, and the market is pricing them as one. Only the first of them fixes itself.

FY26 revenue was $30.0bn, down 0.5% in dollars and 2.4% in constant currency — the first annual dollar decline in TCS’s listed history. Operating margin nonetheless rose to 25.0%, which is the tell: the margin was bought with 23,460 fewer people, not with better pricing. When hiring resumed in Q1 FY27 (+9,279), margin fell 130 basis points to 24.0% in a single quarter.

Order book looks reassuring — $40.7bn of TCV in FY26, $9.5bn in Q1 FY27. It should not reassure us. The same scope of work is now signing for fewer dollars than it did two years ago. A healthy book-to-bill on a deflating rate card is not growth; it is running to stand still.

Meanwhile Infosys grew 3.1% in constant currency on a $20.2bn base in the same year TCS shrank 2.4%. That five-and-a-half point gap is not the cycle. Everyone faced the same clients, the same tariffs, the same AI. TCS is, for the first time in its modern history, the slowest-growing of the large Indian caps at scale.

The threat to TCS is not artificial intelligence. It is that TCS sells effort, and AI is collapsing the amount of effort each unit of client outcome requires.

That distinction matters because it determines the remedy. If the problem were AI capability, TCS would fix it by hiring AI engineers — it already has 270,000 people with AI competencies and a $2.6bn annualised AI book growing 13.6% a quarter. If the problem is the pricing model, then every productivity gain TCS achieves is a discount it hands to the client, and capability alone makes the arithmetic worse, not better.

02 · The record

What five years of numbers actually say

Growth has decelerated every year since FY23 and turned negative in FY26. The rupee has been masking it.

Three numbers from the same period are worth holding together. Revenue per employee rose from $49,640 in FY25 to $51,354 in FY26 — AI leverage is real and it is showing up. Headcount fell 23,460. And total shareholder payout was ₹39,571 crore against free cash flow of ₹42,983 crore — a 92% payout, consistent with a stated policy of returning 80–100% of free cash flow.

That last figure is the one that should concern this office most. There is no retained buffer. TCS distributes essentially everything it generates. Any fall in free cash flow passes through to Tata Sons within one dividend cycle, undamped.

03 · Decomposition

Three forces, wrongly treated as one

Conflating these is how boards get the response wrong. Each has a different half-life and a different remedy.

Force one · Cyclical

Discretionary spend is frozen

Clients in banking, retail and communications have stretched decision cycles and deferred programmes. Tariffs, Middle East disruption and US federal contraction have all bitten. Accenture cut its FY26 growth guidance from 5% to 4% and saw bookings fall 2% year on year; its stock dropped 18% in a session.

Half-life: 4–8 quarters. This one reverses on its own. It is also the smallest of the three.

Force two · Deflationary

The unit of sale is shrinking

AI reduces the hours required per unit of delivered outcome. Under time-and-materials and FTE-priced contracts, that reduction is refunded to the client at renewal. Industry reporting is consistent: the same scope now translates into a smaller dollar value than a year ago, even as order books grow.

Half-life: permanent. This does not reverse. It is only survivable by changing what is being sold and how it is priced.

Force three · Competitive

The work is moving elsewhere

India’s global capability centres are now a $98–100bn market across 2,100+ centres and some 2.3 million people — more than three times TCS’s entire revenue, growing at roughly 20%. Separately, OpenAI has put over $4bn behind placing forward-deployed engineers inside enterprises and acquired a consultancy to do it.

Half-life: a decade. Clients are building the capability TCS used to rent them.

One genuine tailwind deserves naming. The $100,000 H-1B fee, which reads as a headwind, is largely already absorbed: TCS took on roughly 500 new H-1B holders this year and has shifted decisively to local hiring. Analysts put the incremental cost under the old delivery model at about $50m a year — material but not structural. Rivals more dependent on visa-linked onsite staffing carry more of that cost than TCS does.

04 · Demand

Demand is not falling. It is moving.

The revenue line hides three demand pools travelling in different directions at different speeds. Reading them as one budget is how the response gets mis-sized.

Aggregate revenue fell 2.4% in constant currency. No single client cut 2.4% of their spend. What happened is that one pool of demand kept its volume and lost its price, a second pool froze outright, and a third grew quickly from a small base — and the three roughly cancelled.

The money has changed buying centre

This is the part that does not show up in a revenue bridge. Enterprise technology spend is authorised from three different budgets, they are not growing at the same rate, and TCS is not equally well placed in each.

The fastest-growing pool of enterprise technology spend is the one where we have the weakest relationship. The AI budget is not a line item in the CIO’s plan, and it is not being spent with the CIO’s incumbent vendor.

There is a real consolation in the middle row. Cost pressure at the client is demand for TCS — when a chief financial officer mandates a 15% reduction in run-cost, somebody has to be paid to deliver it, and an incumbent with the estate knowledge is the cheapest route. That pool has held up, and it is part of why revenue fell 2.4% rather than 10%. It is also, unavoidably, a pool that shrinks as it succeeds.

By vertical

By geography

India is now the fastest-growing market TCS serves — the India business rose 7.6% sequentially in Q1 FY27. Much of that growth is TCS building and operating global capability centres for international clients. That is real revenue, and it is TCS being paid to construct the thing that will eventually displace it. Both statements are true and the board should hold them together.

05 · Competitive landscape

Losing on the dimensions that are about to matter

TCS is the largest of the Indian majors, the most profitable at scale, and now the slowest-growing. Only two of those three are durable.

The cleanest comparison is the one already made: Infosys grew 3.1% in constant currency on a $20.2bn base in the year TCS shrank 2.4% on $30.0bn. Same clients, same tariffs, same AI, same currency. Five and a half points of difference that the cycle does not explain.

Accenture is the more instructive case, because it is the best-positioned incumbent in the industry and it was punished anyway: FY26 growth guidance cut from 5% to 4%, bookings down 2% year on year, and an 18% fall in a single session. The read-across is not that Accenture is in trouble. It is that the market is not yet discriminating between services firms that will survive AI and those that will not — it is repricing the whole category. It will start discriminating, and TCS should want to be on the right side of that when it does.

Accenture’s advantage over us is not AI capability. It is sequence — it is in the room when the client decides what to do, not just who builds it.

That distinction is worth more now than at any point in twenty years. When the build was expensive, being the builder was the valuable seat. If AI collapses the cost of the build, the advisory seat upstream captures the margin and the build becomes the commodity. Accenture has spent two decades and a great deal of acquisition money buying that seat. TCS has not, and cannot buy it quickly.

Where each competitor actually stands

What we are genuinely good at, stated fairly

Three things, and they are not small. Cost base: at 25% operating margin on $30bn, TCS runs the most efficient delivery organisation of its size anywhere. Incumbency: 66 accounts above $100m, decades of estate knowledge, and security and regulatory clearances a new entrant cannot replicate on any relevant timescale. Disclosed AI scale: a $2.6bn annualised AI book growing 13.6% a quarter is roughly 8.5% of revenue, and most peers do not publish a comparable figure at all. On the honest metric, TCS is not behind.

One number in our own materials should stop being cited to this board, though. The claim of 270,000 people with AI competencies is self-defined, unaudited, and measures training completion rather than deployed capability. It is a reasonable internal mobilisation metric. It is not evidence of competitive position, and presenting it as such invites exactly the challenge we would not survive.

06 · Industry structure

Every force is moving against the incumbent at once

Industry structure usually changes slowly enough that a large firm can adapt inside the drift. What is unusual here is that all five forces have turned in the same direction within about two years.

The supply side: the pyramid is the business model

TCS employs 584,519 people. The economics have never really been about the headcount — they are about its shape. A large base of low-cost engineers billed at a multiple of their cost is what funds the pyramid above them. AI attacks that base first, because entry-level work is the most codified and the most automatable.

This produces a result that is counter-intuitive and important: removing the bottom of the pyramid makes the average unit of delivery more expensive, not less. The cheap layer disappears, the expensive layers remain, and revenue per employee rises while cost per employee rises faster. FY26 showed the first half of that — revenue per employee up 3.5% to $51,354. The second half arrives when the mix shift works through the wage bill.

FY26 also demonstrated that the cost lever is a withdrawal, not a strategy. Margin rose to 25.0% on 23,460 fewer people. The moment hiring resumed in Q1 FY27 — +9,279 — margin fell 130 basis points to 24.0% in a single quarter. That can be done once. It cannot be done every year, and the severe scenario in section 09 requires doing it five years running, to the tune of roughly 250,000 people, in a country where TCS is a politically significant employer.

The visa question is the one supply-side item that has been over-weighted. The $100,000 H-1B fee reads as a headwind but is largely absorbed: TCS took on roughly 500 new H-1B holders this year, has shifted decisively to local hiring, and analysts put the incremental annual cost at about $50m — material, not structural. Rivals more dependent on visa-linked onsite staffing carry proportionally more of it than TCS does.

07 · Unit economics

What actually happens to a contract

Everything in this assessment reduces to one mechanism, and it is worth setting out arithmetically rather than asserting it.

Take a contract worth $100 a year, delivered at a 70% cost ratio, and assume AI reduces the effort required to deliver it by 30% over five years. The delivery saving is identical in all three cases below. What differs is who keeps it — and that is decided entirely by how the contract was priced.

The three lines end at $70, $78 and $112 on identical delivery economics. That spread is not a forecasting difference. It is a contracting difference, and it is the single largest controllable variable in this assessment — larger than margin discipline, larger than the AI capability build, larger than anything in the cost line.

We do not know what share of our revenue is priced on effort. It is the most important number in the business and it is not in any pack that reaches this board.

The seven-bucket model in the next section applies a per-bucket price pass-through assumption to stand in for this mix. That is the right modelling choice given what is disclosed, but it is standing in for a fact that exists and is knowable. Until it is measured, every scenario here carries a wider error bar than it needs to.

08 · Risk register

Where the exposure actually sits

TCS has not disclosed a service-line revenue split since FY21. What follows is a reconstruction — and it is the single most challengeable input in this whole assessment.

$12.7bn — 42% of FY26 revenue — sits in work that AI can substantially automate. Whether that becomes lost revenue depends entirely on two things TCS partly controls: how much of the productivity gain is passed to the client in price, and how far cheaper delivery expands the volume of work clients are willing to buy. That is the whole scenario question, and it is what the model below flexes.

Note what is not exposed. Engineering and R&D services, and products and platforms, together are about 14% of revenue and carry the lowest automation exposure. Products and platforms — BaNCS, ignio, iON, ADD — are the only part of the book whose value AI raises rather than deflates, because their revenue is licensed, not billed by the hour. They are 5% of revenue. That number is a strategic failure a decade in the making, and it is now the most valuable thing in the portfolio.

10 · The Tata Sons view

The concentration is the risk

TCS could perform respectably and still leave this office short, because the parent has committed to a capital programme sized against a dividend that has already started falling.

In FY26 Tata Sons received ₹28,291 crore from TCS, down 12% from ₹32,184 crore the year before. Total dividend income across every holding was ₹32,528 crore. TCS was 87% of it. The rest of a listed portfolio worth ₹11.68 trillion contributed ₹4,237 crore.

Against that, the commitments: the Dholera fab at ₹91,000 crore and Assam assembly at ₹27,000 crore; Air India’s 600-aircraft order and a business into which ₹22,618 crore has already gone; Agratas’s £4bn Somerset plant; Tata Digital, carrying ₹22,903 crore of equity and a ₹4,974 crore FY26 loss. The four newest businesses lost roughly ₹30,000 crore between them last year. Parent net cash at the FY26 close was ₹21,841 crore, with no borrowings.

In the bear case Tata Sons runs a cumulative deficit of ₹19,163 crore across FY27–FY31 and its net cash is all but exhausted. In the severe case the deficit is ₹56,517 crore and the parent is in net borrowing from FY30. The gap between the best and worst cumulative dividend from TCS over five years is ₹1,25,649 crore — roughly the whole cost of the Dholera fab plus the Assam facility.

There is a regulatory dimension to this that should not wait. Tata Sons remains on the RBI’s upper-layer NBFC list, and the application to deregister as a core investment company is still unresolved. If the shock absorber for a falling dividend is access to debt markets at scale, that question needs an answer before it is needed, not after.

We are not exposed to whether TCS grows. We are exposed to having assumed it would.

11 · Sensitivity

What TCS has to do to keep the group whole

Two levers determine almost the whole answer: how fast revenue grows, and what margin survives. Move them and see where FY31 lands.

Run the sliders and the shape of the problem shows itself. Over the range actually available to management, margin cannot rescue a growth failure. Moving operating margin from 25% to 27% — an outcome nobody has achieved in a decade — is worth roughly ₹2,400 crore of FY31 dividend from a flat-revenue base. Getting from flat revenue to 4% growth is worth about ₹6,900 crore, and unlike margin it compounds every year rather than resetting.

Worse, the two are not independent. In every downside path modelled here margin falls because growth fails, not alongside it: idle capacity, pricing concessions to hold volume, and the cost of a workforce that cannot be reshaped as fast as revenue moves. Cost discipline buys time. It does not buy the outcome.

13 · Monitoring

What would change this view

Leading indicators, in order of signal value

  • Revenue per employee. The single cleanest test of whether AI-led delivery is working. If it rises faster than revenue falls, the transition is being managed. FY26: +3.5%.
  • TCV-to-revenue conversion by deal vintage. A growing order book converting to less revenue is the deflation thesis proving itself. This is internal data; we should be seeing it.
  • Renewal pricing on the top 50 accounts. Where the productivity refund actually happens. Watch rate-card movement, not headline wins.
  • BFSI constant-currency growth. 32% of revenue and the first vertical to move in any recovery. FY26: +1.0%.
  • Share of revenue on non-effort-linked pricing. Not currently disclosed. It should be, at least to this board — section 07 shows it is worth a 60-point spread on an identical contract.
  • Grow-pool share of total revenue. The consulting, data, AI, cyber and product estate is 20% of revenue and has to carry the whole transition. If it is not above 30% by FY29 the base case is not being delivered.
  • Win rate against Accenture on advisory-led deals. The sequence problem in section 05 shows up here first, long before it shows up in revenue.
  • Revenue per head in the bottom two grades. The pyramid test. If the base is thinning faster than its revenue, the inversion in section 06 has begun.

What I would need to sharpen this

  • Actual service-line revenue and gross margin. The seven-bucket split here is a reconstruction. Real MIS would materially change the risk quantification, in either direction.
  • Pricing model mix. Time-and-materials versus fixed-price versus outcome-linked, and the trend. This determines how much deflation is contractually unavoidable.
  • The HyperVault business case. Hurdle rate, capital schedule, ring-fencing, exit.
  • Tata Sons’ committed versus discretionary capital call by year. The model treats it as fixed. How much of it genuinely is?
  • Client concentration and renewal calendar. Which of the 66 hundred-million-dollar accounts come up for renewal, and when.
  • Actual vertical and geographic revenue split. Section 04 reconstructs both. TCS has the real numbers and has not published them since FY21.
  • Deal win/loss by competitor. The competitive matrix in section 05 is scored on structural position because win/loss data is not public. It exists internally.
On the confidence of these numbers The financial history, the Tata Sons dividend figures and the capital commitments are from public filings and are reliable. So are the peer data points quoted directly: Infosys at $20.2bn and +3.1% constant currency, Accenture’s guidance cut and bookings decline, the GCC market size, and the TCS figures on headcount, revenue per employee, AI book and total contract value.

Everything else is judgement, and the newer sections contain more of it, not less. Specifically: the seven-bucket revenue decomposition and its per-bucket deflation and volume drivers; the scenario probabilities; the forward capital-call profile; the 60/40 split of application work between run and change in section 04; the vertical and geographic shares in section 04, which are reconstructions of a mix TCS has not disclosed since FY21; the three buying-centre pool sizes and growth rates, which are estimates and are labelled as such; every score in the competitive matrix in section 05 and every rating in the five forces in section 06, which are my assessments and carry no data behind them; and the effort, pass-through and repricing assumptions behind the contract arithmetic in section 07.
